# Hallucination Hysteria — current story and game brief

Micah's story direction overrides earlier key-based and familiar-place story anchors. Keep words on screen to a minimum. No tutorial and no ghost notes.

## Why the Walker is out
It's 2:47 a.m. The Walker, a young person in a hoodie with headphones on, is walking home after another late night on a project they've been pushing for days and still aren't sure they got right. They haven't really slept in days. The walk should take ten minutes.

## What's unquestionably real
The beat in their headphones. It never changes speed and never lies, and the arrows ride on it. Everything else might not be real: the streetlights, the signs, the Stranger, even the Walker's own reflection. Don't use a house key or familiar places as anchors; the surroundings are exactly what the Walker can't trust.

Implementation: each recording plays at its original constant speed. Distortion never changes audio time or arrow timing. The supplied tracks have different tempos between chapters (91 / 91 / 115 BPM); no tempo bending within a song.

## What the Walker is unsure of
Whether what they see is really happening, whether it's the lack of sleep, and whether they're doing anything right, tonight or in the work they just left. The game never answers. Keep the cause of the hallucinations a mystery: the tiredness is a fact, but nothing confirms it's the reason. The mood is uneasy but never helpless: the world is unreliable, but the beat is always there to hold on to.

## How it unfolds
- Opening (Arrive): the Walker comes to mid-step on a dim street, unsure how long they've been walking. Everything is blurred except the beat. Pressing on the beat brings the street back into focus, one streetlight at a time. Start enters the first song directly; this is its opening, not a separate tutorial.
- Stage 1, The street (Wonder): the streetlights flicker in time and seem to sing patterns at the Walker. Copying them keeps the street steady. Small things are off: the OPEN sign's N is backwards, and the clock reads 2:47 every time you look.
- Stage 2, The Stranger (Doubt): someone at the bus stop says the Walker's name in beeps. The Walker can't tell whether they know this person, or whether anyone is there at all. Answering on the beat brings the face into focus for a moment, never long enough to recognize. The bus never comes; the Stranger points up the street toward home. Who they are stays a mystery.
- Stage 3, Your room (Change): home at last, but the room won't settle. The Walker's reflection sings first, like the doubts they've carried all night, and the Walker has to keep their own rhythm against it.
- Clear ending (Leave): morning light. The mirror shows only the Walker, who takes off their headphones. The questions aren't answered, but the beat held and they made it home. "I made it home."
- Lost ending (Leave): the Reflection keeps singing and the Walker goes quiet. "Was any of it real?" Then it's 2:47 a.m. on the blurred street again, with Start Again.

## Exact narrative words on screen
Only title, transitions and endings carry narrative text; gameplay has no dialogue captions.
- Title: "2:47 a.m. A ten-minute walk home. Keep the beat."
- After Stage 1: "The streetlights go quiet. Were they ever singing? Start when you are ready."
- After Stage 2: "The bus never came. The Stranger is gone. Start when you are ready."
- Clear: "I made it home."
- Lost: "Was any of it real?"

Necessary controls, status and failure instructions remain readable. Failure waits indefinitely for Restart. Both ending buttons return to Ready; no forced credits interstitial.

## Revised difficulty and presentation
Every real arrow must be hit; no decoys or sound-only directions. Stage 3 alternates quarters with short eighth-note clusters, with fewer holds. Miss penalty is −4 in Stage 3 and −6 in Stages 1–2. Perfect ±70ms gives +3, Good ±150ms +2, completed hold +2. Each stage starts at 50. Above 50 at the end gives Clear, otherwise Lost.
Arrow-shaped pulses, expanding outlines and directional sparks replace square hit outlines. Reduced motion uses a static highlight.
Retain realistic scenes but simplify distracting detail and glossy lighting. Generated imagery stays credited as such.
